About Me

Using mathematics to understand complex systems, from infectious diseases to human behavior.

My path into mathematics began with a simple fascination: how can a few symbols on a page describe phenomena as complicated as an epidemic, a social network, or an ecosystem?

Over time, that curiosity led me from pure mathematical questions to applied problems at the intersection of mathematics, biology, and the social sciences. What I enjoy most about applied mathematics is its ability to connect seemingly unrelated ideas. The same mathematical framework can help explain how diseases spread, how individuals make decisions, or how populations evolve through time.

Today, I work on mathematical models that seek to better understand complex biological and social systems. I am especially interested in situations where individual behavior influences collective outcomes, and where mathematics can provide insight into problems with real-world consequences.

Why I Do Research

For me, research is a process of asking questions rather than collecting answers. I enjoy exploring problems that sit between disciplines, where mathematical theory meets data, biology, public health, or social behavior.

Some of my recent work has focused on infectious disease dynamics, behavioral effects in disease transmission, optimal intervention strategies, and social interactions in biological systems. What connects these projects is a common goal: understanding how local interactions generate large-scale patterns.

Teaching

Teaching has always been one of the most rewarding parts of academic life. I enjoy helping students develop confidence in mathematics and discover that mathematical thinking extends far beyond the classroom.

My goal as an instructor is not only to teach techniques but also to encourage curiosity, critical thinking, and problem-solving skills that students can apply throughout their careers.

Beyond Mathematics

One of the aspects I value most in academia is community. Throughout my time as a student and researcher, I have been involved in student organizations, leadership roles, and initiatives that support collaboration among scholars from different backgrounds.

These experiences have reinforced my belief that good science depends on communication, mentorship, and the willingness to learn from people working outside our own disciplines.

A Few Things I Believe

  • Mathematics is most powerful when it helps us understand real-world problems.
  • The best research often happens at the boundaries between disciplines.
  • Teaching and research strengthen one another.
  • Curiosity is more important than having all the answers.
  • Good ideas can come from anywhere.
